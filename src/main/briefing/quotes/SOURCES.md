# The bundled phrases and where they come from

Every phrase bundled with Selida is a passage of a public-domain text in a public-domain
translation. The rule for inclusion: the author died before 1955 **and**, for a translation,
the translator died before 1955 or the edition was published before 1930.

## How each entry was made

No passage was typed from memory. For each source the plain text was downloaded from Project
Gutenberg, its own title page was read to confirm the work, the translator and the edition,
and each passage was then copied out of that file by a script, from a first anchor to a last
one. The script refuses a passage it cannot find exactly once in the file, one that does not
start and end at a sentence boundary, one longer than 420 characters or four sentences, and one
that contains a footnote marker.

Two things are changed by that script and nothing else: whitespace (the line wraps of prose
become spaces; verse keeps its line breaks), and Project Gutenberg's `_underscores_` that mark
italics, which are removed. Spelling, punctuation and wording are those of the edition — "thou"
included.

Publication years and the years translators died are stated from general reference knowledge
where the file itself does not print them; the table says which basis applies.

## What is deliberately not here

- **Hermann Hesse** (died 1962). His works are in copyright in Brazil and the European Union
  until the end of 2032, and nearly all English translations are in copyright in their own
  right. None of his text is bundled. Add the passages you love to your own phrases instead
  (Settings → Today → Your own phrases): they are yours to keep on your own computer.
- Any twentieth-century teacher or writer still in copyright: Thich Nhat Hanh, D. T. Suzuki,
  Krishnamurti, Camus, Jung, Alan Watts, Rilke in modern translations, and so on.
- Seneca in Richard Gummere's Loeb translation (1917–25): the edition is old enough for the
  rule above, but the translator died in 1969, so the older Aubrey Stewart translations are
  used instead.
- Sayings attributed to the Buddha that are not in a canonical text. The Buddhist entries are
  verses of the Dhammapada in F. Max Müller's translation, and nothing else.
- Sources whose translator or edition could not be established from the file itself (Pascal,
  Aristotle, Brother Lawrence, Meister Eckhart), or whose text carried too many editorial
  marks to quote cleanly (Julian of Norwich, Nietzsche's *Zarathustra*).

The Analects of Confucius are filed under the "Taoism and the Chinese classics" tradition.
